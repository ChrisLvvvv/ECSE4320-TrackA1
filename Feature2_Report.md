Feature 2: Scheduler Behavior Under Thread Oversubscription

This study examines the behavior of the Linux Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS) when the degree of thread-level parallelism exceeds the available core capacity, a condition known as thread oversubscription. While modern schedulers are designed for scalability, oversubscription introduces significant overhead from increased context switching and resource contention. The experiment was conducted on Ubuntu 22.04 under WSL2 using a custom multithreaded C benchmark, where each thread executes a fixed, compute-bound loop of integer operations. The system reports 20 logical CPUs. Aggregate throughput (Gops/s) and total execution time (s) were measured as the thread count was varied from 1 up to 60.

The performance profile exhibits a clear transition as the system moves from under-utilization to the oversubscribed regime. As shown in the throughput results, scalability is strong until approximately 15 threads, where throughput peaks near 15.5 Gops/s. This region reflects the expected near-linear performance gains as threads efficiently utilize the available cores. As the thread count approaches and slightly exceeds the 20 logical CPUs reported by the system, the scheduler reaches a scalability tipping point. Beyond this threshold, throughput growth diminishes significantly and stabilizes in the range of 16 to 17 Gops/s for thread counts up to 60.

This saturation in throughput is directly complemented by a sharp increase in execution time. Once the thread count exceeds core capacity, total runtime rises rapidly. For example, execution with 60 threads takes approximately 3.5 times longer than execution with 15 threads (about 0.88 s versus 0.25 s). This behavior indicates that scheduling overhead, queuing delays, and frequent context switching dominate execution time in the oversubscribed regime.

A comparison between the unpinned (none) and pinned (taskset) configurations reveals minimal divergence. While pinning offers marginal improvements at specific oversubscription levels, such as a slightly higher throughput around 30 threads, the overall performance trends remain nearly identical. This observation suggests that once the workload becomes context-switch bound, CPU affinity cannot mitigate the intrinsic cost of thread multiplexing. The scheduler must proportionally share available core time among runnable threads regardless of explicit pinning constraints.

Finally, similar to Feature 1, the virtualization layer of WSL2 imposes an important limitation. The logical CPUs presented to the Linux guest are abstractions mapped onto virtual CPUs managed by the Windows host. As a result, the observed oversubscription behavior reflects a combination of Linux CFS scheduling decisions and resource management performed by the host hypervisor. Despite this abstraction, the observed throughput saturation and near-linear growth in execution time accurately reflect the fundamental principles of scheduler contention. These results highlight the importance of matching thread pool size to available physical parallelism in order to achieve efficient and scalable performance.
